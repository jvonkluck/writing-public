# Jordan von Kluck

v0.2.0 2026-10-12

## Who I Am

I have a standard line which I lead my introduction with at the top of every interview I conduct. "Hi, I'm Jordan. I'm a software engineer by trade and training." My first job was as a software tester at the age of 14 and I've been in this space since. The reality is that it's a pursuit which satisfies two of my core drivers: I'm naturally curious and I love solving problems. Choosing to pursue Computer Science in college (university), particularly at the school I attended which focused heavily on a broad engineering curriculum, equipped me with better tools to approach most varieties of problems.

In most situations these days, you probably won't pick it out but I'm an introvert who has learned to be an extrovert. I was genuinely awful at public speaking until Covid when I could simply talk into a laptop, at which point something clicked and it just stopped bothering me. Quiet time to recharge, reflect, and process is critical.

Although it might seem somewhat at odds with this, I care deeply about people. One of the most satisfying elements of the work I've done over the course of my career is creating an environment where talented folks can grow into roles which realize their potential. My time at tastytrade and IG was foundational to growing into the leader, technologist, product person, and human that I am today. We produced some amazing success stories, with folks having gone on to bigger roles inside of the company or moving on to absolutely incredible opportunities elsewhere which we helped to prepare them for. 

Over time I developed a mantra about being responsible for a team: "hire very smart people, understand market comp and ensure folks are paid well, make sure you have interesting projects, and give them the autonomy to do the job". Magic tends to happen when we can line those things up correctly.

I've been an individual athlete for most of my life. Yesterday's PR is the today's new normal. When I do compete in a race or a team sport, a different side of me comes out which absolutely hates to lose. Work can cross into that space, but it's us vs. the rest of the world. It's not a competition inside of our walls.

## What I Bring

As an extension of the above base layer, I'm a builder. Finding elegant solutions to complicated problems and choosing the right set of compromises for a specific situation is deeply satisfying. We approached the build of tastytrade from an engineering perspective and although some of it looked familiar as a brokerage, there were elements of it which stood out in their design as distinctly different from established art in the industry.  

Applying the engineering tool set I mentioned previously to scale up from a few thousand customers at launch to hundreds of thousands of customers was a fairly obvious application of that background and training. (It certainly wasn't simple and involved a number of very challenging periods of growth.) But the same systems thinking approach helped me to develop organizational framework which allowed that team to scale successfully from the initial 15 person team to around 130 engineers and product folks. I strongly believe the structure which helps to run a team at scale also maps out the path for people to grow professionally and provides their managers a framework to support them on that journey.

My years at Lou Malnati's were highly formative in helping to develop strong commercial sensibility. Restaurants are an absolutely brutal business with complex controllable variables, a large and challenging labor force, and thin profit margins. I learned about real customer service, genuine customer focus, and fiscal discipline from some incredible operators. 

Combining structured thinking as informed by an engineering mindset with disciplined commercial leadership provides a useful baseline for product management. My product management philosophy is strongly rooted in the notion that a great product manager internalizes the broader context of an organization (ambitions, customers, economics, competitive landscape) and makes educated trade-offs about the right things to build right now. Decisions are rooted in data, aligned with the stated goals of the organization, and focused on outcomes rather than simply shipping features. I strongly believe in a feedback loop which provides objective feedback to a product team and organizational about the quality of the bets it makes.

I've been through both sides of M&A, participating in deal decks and supporting material, being on the receiving end of diligence inquiries, and played a significant part in the acquisition of tastytrade by IG Group. In the following years I've been on the other side of deals - evaluating potential M&A targets from technology, product, and commercial perspectives.

As someone who has spent well over a decade in a heavily regulated industry and nearly half of that time in a publicly listed company, I'm very comfortable navigating regulatory structures and control frameworks. There's an art to crafting policy which covers a firm's obligations in simple language but still gives you freedom within a framework and designing a group of entities which appropriately firewall the concerns of one and its regulatory coverage from those which should be insulated away.

## How I Work

I read voraciously as a child and still prefer to consume long-form writing as opposed to watching a video or listening to audio. It's faster and I process information more efficiently when it's visual. Similarly (as you've probably inferred at this point), I do a lot of work as longer form writing. 

I like to come to meetings prepared, which usually means that I'll push for an agenda and pre-reads for meetings I'm invited to. I'll typically defer a face to face conversation or meeting until we can pull the necessary data together to operate on facts instead of opinions. When I'm the host, I'll always strive to provide that same level of preparation.

It's common for me to ask for an asynchronous exchange or two in order to capture context and flesh out details before getting on a call. It's probably a bit of the introvert in me, but it's definitely in service of not spending time just being briefed by someone to get to a decision. Conversations where folks are up to speed on context and jump straight into productive discussion are energizing in a unique way.

I'm direct, sometimes to the point of being blunt. I'll always aim to be polite and respectful, but I'll always question inconsistencies or erroneous conclusions. I don't have a strong need to be right, but I want the decisions I'm a part of to be the right ones with the information we have at that moment in time.

I structure my time to optimize for focus, typically getting deep work done early in the morning before the day starts for everyone else and blocking time during the workday for longer periods without interruption when possible. As an outgrowth of this emphasis on focus, I don't check email particularly frequently during the workday and instead batch it with a triage and respond later approach. I'm more responsive to messaging apps like Slack, but often times I'll read and acknowledge messages and wait on a response to more involved conversations until I can give them the time they deserve.

Ethics are a non-negotiable. Running a business which is fundamentally extractive on the backs of its customers or operating in a gray area aren't things I'm interested in.   

## An Eye Toward The Future

At the time of updating this in late 2026, we're in the midst of the world figuring out what adopting AI actually looks like. The hype cycle is real and there's a variety of emotions which range from excitement to fear, existential dread, and angst. I believe that we're best served to take a deeper look at LLMs and agentic capability, understand what it's actually capable of, what it fails at, and ultimately use it as simply the next wave of tools. In the brief history since humans have invented and worked with computers, and the even more brief history in which I've been doing this, we've continually moved the interface up and away from the raw hardware and the bits it understands. This next wave of capability is another step in that direction, but it's not an actual genie that solves every problem for us.

However, I can confidently state is that the pace of change is accelerating exponentially right now. It's a distinct possibility that the business world stratifies into distinct classes of organizations: those which adapt and those which fall significantly behind in the adoption of the new technology. Young organizations have a distinct advantage in that they can shape themselves around an operating model that embraces agentic tools and relies on them as first class contributors to product development and operations. Existing organizations have a harder challenge in forcing themselves to adapt and "level up" in order to move at a pace commensurate with that of younger, leaner companies starting without any legacy concerns.

I think about this as it relates to the next ten to fifteen years of my career. I worry about getting stale or too entrenched in a specific way of thinking. It's a moment right now where I'm conscious that I need to un-learn some of what's worked really well in the past decade and force myself to explore innovation happening at a small cohort of companies. It's a bit scary, but mostly exciting. And as one of my mentors likes to say: "things usually aren't nearly as bad or nearly as good as they seem at first."
